International Trade

Grade 8 Economics · Interactive Study Guide

📦 What Is International Trade?

Indonesia grows excellent coffee and palm oil but cannot produce commercial aircraft. Germany engineers world-class machinery but cannot grow rice. So they trade. International trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries — selling what you produce best (exports) and buying what others produce better (imports).

Comparative Advantage: Even if a country could make everything itself, it makes more sense to specialise in what it does best and trade for the rest. Indonesia focuses on palm oil; Japan focuses on electronics. Both end up better off.

📖 Key Terms — Click to Expand

Export ▼
Goods or services sold to another country.
🇮🇩 Indonesia exports palm oil to India and the EU.
Import ▼
Goods or services bought from another country.
🇮🇩 Indonesia imports aircraft from the USA and machinery from Germany.
Trade Surplus ▼
When a country exports more than it imports — more money flowing in than out. Indonesia often runs a surplus in commodities.
Trade Deficit ▼
When a country imports more than it exports — more money flowing out than in.
Tariff ▼
A tax placed on imported goods, making them more expensive. Used to protect local industries from foreign competition.
Quota ▼
A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. e.g. "Only 10,000 foreign cars allowed per year."
Free Trade ▼
Trade between countries with no tariffs, quotas, or barriers. Example: the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA) among Southeast Asian nations.
Balance of Trade ▼
The difference between a country's exports and imports. Positive = surplus; negative = deficit.

In the Trade Game, student groups represent countries with different resources (paper, scissors, rulers, pens). Each country tries to "manufacture" goods (cut shapes) and sell them to the World Bank for points. To produce more, countries must trade with each other.

📊 Terms of Trade

The terms of trade is the ratio of export prices to import prices. If your export prices rise while import prices stay the same, your terms of trade improve — you get more for what you sell.

This is why Indonesia aims to move from exporting raw commodities (low value) to exporting manufactured goods and services (higher value).

🔤 SPICED & WPIDEC

Two mnemonics to organise the advantages and disadvantages / threats of international trade. Click any letter to expand its meaning.

S Specialisation
Countries focus on what they produce most efficiently. Productivity and quality rise for everyone involved in trade.
🇮🇩 Indonesian batik artisans have centuries of skill — exporting this earns more than competing in industries Indonesia has no advantage in.
P Price
Global competition pushes prices down for consumers. More supplier countries = more competition = cheaper goods.
📱 Smartphones are affordable partly because global competition among manufacturers drives down prices.
I Income & Innovation
Export revenues generate income for businesses, workers, and governments. Exposure to global competition drives companies to innovate.
🇮🇩 Indonesia earns billions in foreign exchange from coal and palm oil, funding schools and infrastructure.
C Choice
Consumers gain access to a far wider range of goods than any single country could produce alone.
🛒 Without trade, Indonesians couldn't easily access Japanese electronics, Australian beef, or French cosmetics.
E Employment
Export industries create jobs. When a country sells more abroad, factories and farms hire more workers.
👗 Indonesia's textile and garment industry employs millions producing clothing for global brands.
D Development
Trade allows developing countries to grow their economies, raise living standards, and access new technologies and skills.
📈 Export-led growth lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty in East and Southeast Asia over the past 50 years.
W Workers' Exploitation
In the race to keep export prices competitive, some countries suppress wages or allow poor working conditions. Workers earn very little while goods sell for much more overseas.
👕 Garment workers in some countries earn a fraction of what a finished piece of clothing sells for in European stores.
P Political Dependency
Heavy reliance on one trading partner creates political vulnerability. If that partner imposes sanctions or withdraws investment, the dependent country suffers badly.
⚠️ A country that exports 70% of its goods to one nation can have its economy used as a political bargaining chip.
I Industry Destruction
Cheap imports can wipe out local industries that can't compete on price. Jobs are lost and skills disappear — sometimes permanently (de-industrialisation).
🏭 Cheap imported textiles have threatened traditional Indonesian weaving industries in some regions.
D Dependency on Commodities
Countries relying heavily on raw material exports are vulnerable to price volatility. When commodity prices crash, the whole economy suffers.
⛏️ Indonesia is a major coal exporter. When global coal prices fall sharply, government revenue and growth take a hit.
E Environmental Damage
The pressure to produce more for export — especially in agriculture and mining — can cause deforestation, pollution, and habitat loss.
🌳 Expanding palm oil plantations for export has contributed to deforestation and loss of orangutan habitat in Borneo and Sumatra.
C Cultural Erosion
A flood of foreign brands, media, and products can overwhelm local culture. Small local businesses struggle to survive against powerful multinationals.
🍔 Global fast-food chains and streaming services can displace local food culture, entertainment, and traditions.

🏭 Multinational Companies (MNCs)

A multinational company (MNC) is a business that operates in more than one country — headquartered in one nation but with factories, offices, or stores in many others.

⚖️ Impacts on Indonesia

💼 Job CreationFactories and offices employ millions of local workers. West Java and Banten are major hubs for MNC-linked manufacturing jobs.
💰 Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)Capital flows in to build factories, pay wages, and fund infrastructure. This money circulates through the broader Indonesian economy.
🔧 Technology & Skills TransferMNCs bring modern equipment and management practices. Local workers gain new skills; local suppliers are pushed to upgrade their standards.
📈 Government Tax RevenueCorporate taxes paid by MNCs contribute to the national budget — funding schools, hospitals, and roads.
📤 Profit RepatriationMNCs send profits back to their home country. The jobs are local, but the wealth ultimately flows out of Indonesia.
🏪 Displacing Local BusinessesLarge MNCs with global supply chains and marketing budgets can drive local warung and Indonesian brands out of the market.
⚖️ Labour ConcernsSome MNCs or their local contractors pay low wages and impose difficult working conditions to keep costs competitive.
🌿 Environmental ExploitationResource-extraction MNCs have been associated with deforestation, river pollution, and land conflicts. The Grasberg mine in Papua is one high-profile example.

🏛️ Indonesia's Policy Response

  • Local content requirements — MNCs must source a % of inputs from Indonesian suppliers
  • Divestment rules — foreign mining companies must gradually sell majority ownership to Indonesian entities
  • Investment negative list — certain sectors are restricted or closed to foreign ownership (e.g. traditional markets, some media)
  • BKPIM — the national investment board screens and approves all foreign investment proposals

🇪🇺 The European Union

After two World Wars fought on European soil, nations made a radical decision: tie your economies together so tightly that war with your neighbour becomes unthinkable. The result is the European Union — a political and economic union of 27 member states, formed from a 1957 treaty between 6 founding nations.

Note: The United Kingdom left the EU in January 2020 in a process known as Brexit, causing significant economic disruption and illustrating how deeply integrated the bloc had become.

🔓 The Four Freedoms of the Single Market

The European Single Market treats all 27 member nations as one economy. Its foundation is four freedoms — click each to learn more.

📦
Free Movement of Goods
No tariffs or customs checks between member states. A French cheese company can sell to Poland with no import tax, as if selling to a city in the next region.
🏦
Free Movement of Services
A company in one EU country can offer services in any other. A Spanish bank can open branches in Germany without special permits.
💶
Free Movement of Capital
Money and investments can flow freely across borders. A Dutch investor can buy shares in an Italian company with no extra restrictions or exchange controls.
🧳
Free Movement of People
EU citizens can live, work, and study in any member state. A Romanian nurse can work in Sweden; a German student can study in Spain — no visa required.
