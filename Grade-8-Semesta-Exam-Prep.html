Q14–16 · Living and Growing · Urban Farming
Read the article carefully, then choose the best answer based on the information in the text.
Living and Growing: The Rise of Urban Farming

In cities around the world, a quiet revolution is taking place on rooftops, balconies, and unused plots of land. Urban farming — the practice of growing food within city boundaries — is becoming increasingly popular as people search for sustainable alternatives to industrial agriculture.

One of the key environmental advantages of urban farming is that it reduces the distance food travels from farm to consumer. In conventional food systems, produce often travels thousands of kilometres before reaching supermarket shelves, consuming large amounts of fuel and generating significant carbon emissions. When vegetables are grown locally, this so-called "food miles" problem is dramatically reduced.

Urban farms also make productive use of otherwise wasted space. In Singapore, vertical farms have been installed on the walls of public housing blocks, producing leafy vegetables year-round using minimal water and no pesticides. In Detroit, USA, a city that lost much of its population due to industrial decline, vacant lots have been transformed into thriving community gardens that now supply fresh produce to local food banks.

However, urban farming is not without its challenges. Land in cities is expensive, and not all buildings are structurally capable of supporting heavy soil and equipment. Water supply can also be an issue, though many urban farmers are addressing this by collecting rainwater or using hydroponic systems that recycle water efficiently.

Despite these obstacles, many urban planners and environmentalists see urban farming as an essential part of the sustainable city of the future. By reconnecting people with the food they eat, it also promotes environmental awareness and a sense of community responsibility.
